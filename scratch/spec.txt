This is the build spec for a two-day demo that proves we can build autonomous agents for
Inizio's SurveyNav platform. It covers the background you need, what to build, in what
order, and what the finished demo has to show. No prior knowledge of market research is
assumed.

Background
Inizio Ignite runs market research for pharmaceutical companies. A drug company wants
to know what doctors think of a new treatment, what would make them prescribe it, or
what price feels acceptable. Inizio designs a survey, sends it to a few hundred doctors or
patients across several countries, and hands back the findings.
A project runs in roughly this order:
1. Write the questionnaire (a Word document, written by a researcher)
2. Program it into survey software — the client uses Confirmit
3. Test it before anyone real sees it
4. Send it out and collect answers — this period is called fieldwork
5. Clean the data: remove people who cheated or rushed
6. Build tables, then charts and a final report
They are building an internal platform called SurveyNav 2.0 to automate the middle of
that chain. Their requirements document lists 29 requirements. We were asked one
question: can we build autonomous agents for them, and have we thought it through
properly. Our answer is a demo plus a written artifact, and this document is the spec for
the demo.

What soft launch means
Rather than send the survey to all 300 people at once, you send it to the first 30 or 50,
stop, and check the data looks sensible before opening it to everyone.

If the survey is broken you find out after 30 wasted responses instead of 300. Without
that pause you discover the problem at the end, when the budget is spent and the only fix
is to run the whole thing again. That is why the client cares about it.
Today a person does this check by eye.

REQ-009, in their words
Automate explicit soft launch QC checks after initial completes are collected,
separate from general QC, Joker review and AE workflows.
Their four acceptance criteria:
Soft launch mode can be triggered at defined completes per market/wave.
Automated checks compare expected versus observed routing, quotas,
terminations, response distributions, missing data and suspicious patterns.
Output is a soft-launch QC checklist/status with issues, severity and go/no-go
recommendation.
Results are separate from general QC/Joker/AE reviews but can reference related
flags.

The same thing in plain English
When enough early responses have arrived, the system should wake up on its own, run a
batch of quality checks over the data, work out what any problems actually mean, and tell
a human whether it is safe to launch the survey to everyone — with the evidence
attached.
Two words in their criteria matter for how we build it. Checklist means the set of checks
must be fixed and repeatable, so they can prove the same checks ran every time. Go/nogo recommendation means the system advises; a human still decides.

What we are building
A program that takes a set of survey responses, finds the problems in them, works out
why each problem is happening, and recommends whether the survey is safe to launch —
with a human approving the final call.

The important part is how it works out the why. A fixed batch of checks finds the
symptoms. Then an AI agent investigates each symptom on its own: it forms a theory,
queries the data to test it, throws the theory away if it is wrong, tries another, and stops
when it is satisfied. Nobody scripts that path. The number of steps is different every time.
That is the whole claim we are proving. Everything below exists to make that visible on
screen.

Architecture

soft-launch QC pipeline · 8 stages, 1 agent

Responses go through a fixed battery of checks. The power gate drops any check the
sample is too small to support. Triage ranks what is left, and one investigator agent is
launched per anomaly. Each investigator can walk the survey model to confirm what it
suspects. The adjudicator collects their conclusions and a human signs off.

Only the highlighted box is an agent
Everything else is ordinary code or a single model call. That distinction matters and we say
it out loud in the client artifact, because the fastest way to lose a technical reviewer is to
draw eight boxes with robot icons on them.
The test we use: can you say how many model calls a component will make before you
run it? The check runner makes zero. Triage makes one. The adjudicator makes one. The
investigator makes somewhere between six and twenty-five, decided as it goes, and that
is what makes it an agent.
So the demo ships one agent type, launched as many times as the data demands. Resist
the urge to inflate that.

Why there are two sample sizes
At n=45 several checks are not statistically valid — you cannot judge whether a
distribution has shifted from 45 people. At n=150 they are. Running both back to back is
how we demonstrate the power gate, which is one of the four things worth protecting.

The fault catalogue is the most important idea here
We declare each fault once, in faults.json . Each entry says two things: what it does to
the data, and what causes it in the survey.
The data generator reads the first half. The survey model reads the second. That is what
makes the closed loop honest — when the agent walks the survey and finds the cause, it
is finding the same fault that produced the symptom, not a staged coincidence. If you

Two of the client's six named check categories are not buildable: routing conformance

Piece 2 — the power gate
Also no AI. This is the differentiator almost nobody builds.
A soft launch has 30 to 50 people. Most distribution-based checks are statistically
meaningless at that size, and a tool that reports them anyway produces noise and loses
trust in its first week. So before running, each check is marked valid or not for the current
sample size.

Piece 3: the investigator agent
This is the only real agent in the demo, and the thing the client is paying attention to.
Everything else exists to set it up.
One investigator is launched per anomaly, in parallel. Each is given one anomaly and told
to find out why it is happening. Nobody tells it how.

Why this is an agent and not a clever prompt
The model is given tools. It calls one, sees the result, decides what to call next, and keeps
going until it concludes. The number of steps is not known before the run — one
investigation might take six calls, another twenty-two. That is the definition we use
throughout the client artifact, so make sure the demo actually behaves this way.
The loop is the standard tool-use loop:
while response.stop_reason == "tool_use":
results = [run_tool(b) for b in response.content if b.type == "tool_use"]
messages.append({"role": "assistant", "content": response.content})
messages.append({"role": "user", "content": results})
response = client.messages.create(model="claude-opus-5-5", tools=TOOLS,
messages=messages, ...)

Use claude-opus-5-5 for the demo. The quality of the reasoning trace is the product
here, and the trace is what the audience reads. Cost is irrelevant at this volume — a whole
run is under two dollars.

Guardrails
Build these on day one, not after something runs away:
Hard cap of 25 tool calls per investigation. If it hits the cap, it reports what it has with
low confidence
Loop detection — the same tool with the same arguments three times means it is
stuck; stop it
Result size cap. A query_data that returns 400 rows will fill the context. Truncate to
the top 20 and say how many were omitted
Timeout per investigation

Memory, in one paragraph
Each investigator keeps only its own conversation. Investigators running in parallel do not
share memory and must not see each other's findings. If one forms a wrong theory, a
shared scratchpad would spread it to the others. Keep them independent and
deduplicate afterwards in the adjudicator. Say this in the client artifact too — it is a small
point that signals real experience.

Getting there will take prompt iteration
Budget real time for this on day 2 — it is where the hours actually go, not in writing the
code. Two things to push for in the system prompt:
State your theory before you test it. Without this the trace is a list of queries and
reads like a script
If a theory does not hold, say so and move on. A visible dead end is one of the four
things we most want on screen, and the model will not produce one unless you ask for
it

Pieces 4 and 5: the survey model and the adjudicator
Piece 4 — the survey model
A small state machine, roughly fifty lines, standing in for the real survey. Around ten
questions, not the full 195.
Do not use a browser. No Playwright, no real Confirmit. In production the walker drives a
real browser, and the client artifact says so plainly. For a live demo a browser is pure risk:
slow, flaky, and dependent on whatever machine you are presenting from. A state
machine runs in milliseconds and never fails in front of an audience.
It needs:
About ten questions, including S2 , S5 , Q7Sk , Q9 and Q12 — the ones the planted
faults touch
Real routing, including the skip that F06 breaks
Two language settings and two device settings
The faults from faults.json encoded as actual behaviour: with language 1046 and
device mobile, Q7Sk returns no input control at all
The walk_survey tool steps through it and returns what a respondent would have
experienced — which questions appeared, what could be entered, where it ended.

The closed loop, and why it is the best thing in the demo
The investigator notices something odd in the data, forms a theory about the survey, and
goes and tests that theory against the survey itself before reporting.
Nobody does this today. A researcher who spots an oddity in the data has to hand it to a
different team to check the script. The loop is closed by an email and two days. Here it
closes in nine seconds, inside one investigation.
Make sure the trace shows it clearly, with the walk result quoted in the final finding. If one
thing gets remembered from the demo, this should be it.

Piece 5 — the adjudicator
A single model call, not an agent. It takes every investigator's finding and produces the
final report.
It must:
Deduplicate. One fault often produces several symptoms. F01 will surface as both item
non-response and a device anomaly; they are one finding, not two
Assign severity — critical, high, medium, low
Produce a go/no-go recommendation with reasoning. This is the client's acceptance
criterion, word for word
Report what was not checked — the power-gate suppressions and the two blocked
categories
The recommendation can be per market. F01 affects two of five languages, so the right
answer is "go for three markets, hold two" — which is more useful than a blanket no, and
shows the system is reasoning about impact rather than counting flags.
Use structured output so the report is data rather than prose you have to parse to score.

Piece 6: scoring against ground truth
Small piece, large effect. It compares the final report against ground_truth_n150.json
and prints a score.
This is what turns a demo into evidence. Anyone can show a system producing output.
Showing that you measured it, against answers known in advance, is what makes a client
believe you have built this kind of thing before.

Diagnosis accuracy is the one to lead with. Spotting that a number looks wrong is easy
and a rules engine does it for free. Working out why is the thing we are claiming an agent
can do, so that is the number that tests the claim.

Report the misses
The agent will miss something. At n=150 it will probably get six or seven of eight. Put the
misses on screen with the reason:
Found 6 of 8. F07 was correctly suppressed by the power gate at this sample size.
F03 was flagged but attributed to device rather than sample source — wrong cause,
counted as a miss.
Resist the urge to tune the demo until it scores 8 of 8. A clean sweep on your own test
data invites the obvious question about whether the test was rigged. A reported miss,
with a reason, answers that question before it is asked and is far more persuasive.

Run it twice
Run at n=45 and again at n=150, and show both. The first demonstrates the power gate
holding things back; the second shows the full investigation. The contrast is the clearest
evidence that the system understands its own limits.

Three things that will trip you up
Capture traces the moment something works. Do not leave recording to the end. The
most common way this goes wrong is a system that worked at 14:00, broke at 16:00 during
tidying, and was never recorded. Save a JSON trace for every run, from the first one.
Day 2 morning is prompt work, not code. The agent will run on day 1 and produce a boring
trace — a flat list of queries with no visible reasoning. Turning that into something worth
watching takes several passes. That is normal. Budget for it rather than being surprised.

The artifact cannot wait for the demo. Everything except the measured numbers should
be written by midday on day 2, with placeholders. If the demo slips, the artifact still ships,
and the artifact is the main deliverable — it is the thing that answers the client's actual
question.

What not to build, and what will cost you hours
Do not build
Playwright or any browser automation. The survey model is a state machine
Anything touching real Confirmit. We have no access and do not need it
More agent types. One investigator. Adding a "planner agent" and a "verifier agent"
that each make one model call is exactly the padding the client artifact warns against,
and it would contradict our own document
Routing conformance or quota checks. Both need inputs that do not exist. Declare
them blocked
A web UI. Terminal output with clear formatting is fine. If there is spare time at the end,
an HTML trace viewer is nice — but it is the last thing, not the first

Gotchas
pip is permission-denied on this machine. The script in Program Files cannot be run.

Use python -m pip install --user <package> — that works. The anthropic SDK is
already installed this way.
ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL is set but there is no API key in the environment. Sort this before

anything else on day 1.
query_data will blow up the context if you let it. A grouped query over 150 respondents

can return hundreds of rows. Cap results at about 20 rows and tell the model how many
were omitted. Without this an investigation dies around step 12 with a context error.
The data is nested. Two loop blocks, LoopT1 and LpPSPrices , are arrays of repeated
question sets. Multi-choice answers are stored as lists of selected and unselected codes,
not booleans. Flatten once, early, and have every tool read the flat version — do not make
the agent deal with the raw shape.
The schema contains no routing logic. Zero mentions of conditions, skips or filters — we
checked. Do not go looking for it. Routing lives in a Word questionnaire the client has not
given us, which is why routing conformance is out of scope.

Two things worth knowing about the client
Their requirements document lists 29 requirements, and 25 of them explicitly require a
human to review, approve or override. The word "autonomy" appears exactly once, in a
question asking how much should be allowed. They are a pharma supplier and they are
cautious about this, with good reason.
So the demo should feel capable, not reckless. The agent investigates entirely on its own;
a human approves the launch decision. That balance is the point, and the client artifact
argues it at length.
